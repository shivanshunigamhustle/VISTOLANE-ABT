## The claim is not the same thing as the proof

Across thirty programme guides on this site, one pattern of pitfall recurs more than any other, phrased almost identically from country to country and route to route: under-evidencing. Not lying, not a fabricated relationship or a false intent — genuinely true circumstances, assessed on paper by someone who was not there, against evidence that fell short of what the true circumstances would actually support. This is worth its own page precisely because it is so easy to misread as a problem only dishonest applicants have. The pitfalls recorded on this site say otherwise.

## Relationships that are real and still under-evidenced

Family routes carry this risk most visibly, because "genuine relationship" is the entire test and the government has no way to assess it except through documents. Canada's spousal sponsorship guide records under-evidencing a genuine relationship as its own pitfall, distinct from any question about whether the relationship is actually genuine. The UK's spouse and partner visa carries a related but more specific version: an unmarried partnership has to show at least two years resembling a marriage before it is assessed on the same footing as one, and a couple together for a shorter period, however genuine, does not yet meet that specific evidentiary bar. Germany's spouse and family reunion route extends the same logic to the practical side of the relationship — under-evidencing accommodation or financial resources is recorded as a pitfall separate from the relationship question entirely, because the requirement is specific and defined, not a general impression of the couple's circumstances.

## Intent that is honest and still unpersuasive

Visitor and student routes ask an applicant to prove something about the future — that they intend to leave, or that they intend to study rather than work — and intent is inherently harder to evidence than a fact. The UK's Standard Visitor visa and Australia's Visitor visa both record under-evidencing genuine visitor or temporary intent as a named pitfall, in each case separate from whether the intent is actually genuine. Australia's Student visa frames the same risk from a different angle: treating the Genuine Student requirement as a formality, when it is in fact assessed on specific evidence of the applicant's individual circumstances, not assumed from a strong academic offer alone.

## Numbers that are accurate and still not enough

Points-based and ranking systems introduce a version of this problem that looks different on the surface but runs on the same logic underneath. Australia's Skilled Independent visa records under-evidencing points-test claims as a distinct pitfall from simply calculating a score incorrectly — a claimed point can be accurate and still fail if the supporting documentation does not meet the specific standard the assessment expects. Canada's Express Entry guide records a related risk from the timing side: starting the evidence-gathering too late, so that by the time a candidate needs to demonstrate a specific criterion, the record that would have proven it cleanly no longer exists in the form the system expects.

## What this pattern actually implies

None of these pitfalls are about applicants misrepresenting their circumstances. Every one of them is about a true circumstance that was not translated into the specific form a government assessor could act on — the right document, the right category of evidence, gathered early enough and specific enough to survive being read by someone who was not there and has no reason to take a claim on trust. The consistent lesson across four countries and every intent type this pattern touches is the same: a genuine case is the necessary condition, not the sufficient one. Evidence has to be built for the case with the same deliberateness as the case itself.
