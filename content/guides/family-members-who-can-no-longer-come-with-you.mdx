## The question that used to have one answer, and now has five

"Can my family come with me, and can they work while they're here" is asked on route after route across this site, and until recently the honest answer on most of them was a fairly stable yes, with conditions. That has stopped being true. Reported changes on at least three of the five countries here have narrowed dependant work rights for a defined category of applicant, in each case abruptly rather than gradually, and a plan built on an older understanding of any of these routes can be wrong through no fault of the applicant's.

## Canada: a right that existed, then did not

The clearest case is dated precisely. As of 21 January 2025, Immigration, Refugees and Citizenship Canada narrowed who qualifies for an open work permit as the family member of a foreign worker or international student. Dependent children of foreign workers no longer qualify for an open work permit under this route at all — not a smaller allowance, a removal. Spouses of international students now generally need the student to be enrolled in a specific graduate or professional programme; many undergraduate and college programmes no longer qualify a spouse for an open work permit where they may have before. Spouses of foreign workers now generally need the principal applicant to hold a job at a senior or specialised skill level, or on a specific occupation list, with a meaningful period of work authorisation remaining. Some exemptions remain, including spouses covered by free trade agreements and spouses already being sponsored for permanent residence.

## The UK: an occupation-level line drawn through the workforce

The UK's change is reported as similarly abrupt and similarly tied to a specific date. For Skilled Worker visas granted on or after 22 July 2025, only workers in roles at RQF Level 6 and above — broadly, graduate-level occupations — can sponsor dependants at all. Workers in medium-skilled roles, RQF 3 to 5, including most roles on the Temporary Shortage List, are reported as no longer able to bring dependants under new applications from that date. Where dependants are permitted, the work rights themselves are reported as broad — dependants of Skilled Worker visa holders can generally work in any job, full-time or part-time, without separate sponsorship, with the narrow exception of professional sport. The gate here is not what a dependant can do once included; it is whether the worker's own occupation clears the line that determines whether a dependant can be included at all. A further change reported from April 2026 would require new adult dependant applicants to demonstrate English at level B2, adding a second, separate bar on top of the occupation line.

## Australia: work rights tied tightly to the visa stage

Australia's partner visa route is reported as more stable in structure but still worth stating precisely, because the timing detail matters. A dependent child included as a secondary applicant is reported receiving the same visa grant as the primary applicant, with the same work and study rights, once the temporary partner visa (subclass 820) stage is granted. For an onshore applicant, a Bridging Visa A is reported issued from the day the application is lodged, carrying full work rights, including for dependent children included in the application — meaning the practical work-rights question turns less on the partner visa's own eligibility and more on correctly understanding what the bridging visa covers in the gap before a decision.

## Germany: broad rights once the residence permit converts, with one exemption worth knowing

Germany's structure is reported as the most permissive of the five once a spouse has actually converted from the entry visa to a residence permit — generally entitled to work without a separate work permit or employment authorisation. The catch is procedural rather than a work-rights restriction: a spouse has a reported window of around 3 months after arrival to convert the entry visa into the residence permit, and the unrestricted work right is tied to that residence permit, not to the original visa. One further exemption is worth knowing specifically: spouses of EU Blue Card holders are reported exempt from the standard A1 German language requirement before entry and receive an unrestricted right to work from the date of the family reunification permit with no waiting period, a materially faster and less conditional path than the general family reunion route.

## Vietnam: the question this site still cannot answer with confidence

Vietnam's DT investor visa route carries the same underlying question — this site's own guide lists whether a spouse or child can be included on the same application or residence card as a still-open FAQ, needing direct confirmation rather than an assumed answer either way. Unlike the other four countries here, no dated policy change was found to report for this route specifically; the honest position is that this is unconfirmed, not that it is settled in either direction.

## What this means before assuming a family member's status

None of this means a spouse, partner or dependent child cannot come, or cannot work. It means the specific answer, for the specific route, at the specific date of application, has moved substantially in at least three of these five countries within roughly the last eighteen months, in each case by drawing a new line — a job's skill level, a student's programme type, a document's conversion window — rather than a general tightening. Working from a friend's experience, an older article, or how the route worked even a year earlier is no longer a safe basis for a family's plan on any of these five routes; the current position has to be checked against the specific worker's occupation, the specific student's programme, or the specific spouse's document status, not assumed from the country's general reputation.
