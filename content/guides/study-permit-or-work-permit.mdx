## The question underneath the question

"Should I study or should I work?" is rarely the real question. The real question is usually one of these: which route can I actually qualify for right now, which one can I afford, and which one gets me closer to staying in Canada long term. A study permit and an employer-sponsored work permit answer those three questions very differently, and confusing them is where most bad plans start.

A study permit is built around an institution and a bank balance. You need a place at a designated learning institution and proof you can fund yourself, and the route is open to a wide range of ages, backgrounds and career stages. A work permit obtained through a Labour Market Impact Assessment is built around an employer and a specific job. You need someone in Canada who has already decided to hire you, and who is willing to spend weeks on recruitment paperwork and pay a government fee to do it. One route depends on you being accepted somewhere. The other depends on someone else wanting to hire you badly enough to go through a process most small employers find unappealing.

## What each route actually asks of you

The study route asks for money up front and patience afterwards. On this site's Canada guide, the confirmed cost-of-living figure for a single applicant outside Quebec is $22,895 CAD as of September 2025, on top of tuition and travel; a threshold you have to evidence before you arrive, not a rough estimate of what the year will cost. Since 22 January 2024, most new post-secondary applicants at the college or undergraduate level also need a provincial or territorial attestation letter, a second approval from the province itself, on top of the institution's acceptance. Two authorities, two separate yeses, before the study permit application is even complete.

The employer-sponsored work route asks for a job offer that is real enough for an employer to defend it. The employer runs recruitment, for a high-wage position, at least three activities including four consecutive weeks of advertising on the Government of Canada Job Bank, finishing within three months of applying, and keeps evidence of every Canadian and permanent resident who applied and why they were not hired. That is before Employment and Social Development Canada even looks at the application. The worker is named in it but does not submit it, pay for it, or control its timing.

## Speed is not what people expect

Neither route is fast, but they are slow in different places. A study permit's processing time varies by country of application and is published as a moving figure, not a promise; no single number applies everywhere, which is precisely why this site's programme page marks it unverified rather than guessing. What is stable is what happens after approval: a study permit is normally valid for the length of the programme plus 90 days, giving a predictable window once you are in.

The work permit route front-loads its delay into the employer's preparation. The recruitment period, weeks of advertising before anyone even applies, is not counted in any published LMIA processing time, so a plan built only on the headline processing figure will run short. Once a positive assessment is issued, the clock is unforgiving in the other direction: for applications received on or after 1 May 2024, the assessment is valid for a maximum of 6 months, inside which the worker must apply for their own work permit. Miss that window and the employer is generally back at the start, fee and recruitment included.

## Who each route actually suits

The study route suits someone who has chosen a programme deliberately, can fund the first year without relying on Canadian income, and is comfortable with a multi-year sequence, study, then work, then possibly permanent residence, rather than an immediate change in status. It suits it badly if the real goal is simply to work as soon as possible: the work allowance during study is capped, and treating a course as the cheapest entry ticket available usually ends in a mismatch between what was paid for and what it delivers.

The employer-sponsored work route suits someone who already has a genuine offer from an employer prepared to do the paperwork, and who wants to be earning in Canada quickly rather than studying first. It suits it badly if there is no real job offer yet, there is no way to obtain a Labour Market Impact Assessment speculatively, and no way to shop one around. It also suits larger or more experienced employers better than very small ones, because the obligations, the advertising, the record-keeping, the exposure to inspection, fall entirely on the business, and a small employer's enthusiasm for a candidate can evaporate on contact with the requirements.

## They are not always alternatives

For a meaningful number of people, this is not actually an either/or choice. Canadian study followed by Canadian work experience is a recognised path toward permanent residence, and time spent on one route can improve eligibility for programmes that reward Canadian experience specifically. Read that way, the study and employer-sponsored routes are sometimes sequential steps in the same plan rather than competing options, which is also why the choice of study programme, and the choice of employer, are decisions worth making with the next step already in mind, not only the immediate one.

## What to check before deciding

Whichever route looks closer to yours, confirm the current, published figures before committing money or time: study permit processing times by country, the current LMIA wage thresholds and stream requirements, and the current provincial attestation allocations, since all three are revised on schedules this article does not control. The programme pages on this site mark exactly which figures are confirmed and which still need checking, and that distinction is the more useful thing to read than either page's headline number.
