## Three ways a work route goes wrong, not one

Work-intent routes on this site carry more recorded pitfalls than any other single intent, sixteen across five countries, and reading them together shows they are not sixteen unrelated problems. They cluster into three: an obligation that belongs to the employer, not the applicant, going unmet; a threshold everyone assumed applied and nobody checked; and a licence, exemption or degree recognition treated as settled when it was still open.

## The employer's obligation, not the applicant's

Canada's LMIA route is the clearest example of a work application that depends on someone else's compliance. Before an employer can even apply, they must complete a defined recruitment process — for a high-wage position, at least four consecutive weeks advertising through the Government of Canada's own Job Bank — and advertising that falls short of this is recorded as its own pitfall. The assessment itself belongs entirely to the employer; a worker cannot obtain one directly, and treating it as something within their own control is recorded as a separate, distinct mistake.

The UK's Skilled Worker visa runs on the same shape: a valid Certificate of Sponsorship from a licensed employer is a hard prerequisite, issued through the employer's own sponsor licence, and applying without one is recorded as a pitfall in its own right. Neither route can be rescued by a strong personal application if the employer's side of the obligation is not in order.

## The threshold that looked generic but was not

A second cluster involves applicants who cleared a headline number and assumed that was the whole test. The UK's Skilled Worker visa sets salary thresholds that vary by occupation — a specific, occupation-linked "going rate" — and assuming any salary above the general published figure qualifies is recorded as a pitfall, because the real bar moves with the specific role. Australia's Skills in Demand visa carries the same risk from the job-offer side: not any job offer qualifies, and assuming otherwise is its own recorded mistake. Germany's EU Blue Card repeats the pattern once more — a job offer above the salary threshold is necessary but the degree itself has to be recognised as comparable to a German qualification, and assuming any degree-holding offer clears the bar is recorded as a pitfall distinct from the salary question.

## A licence, an exemption or a recognition treated as already settled

The third cluster involves something an applicant assumed was resolved that was, in fact, still open. Australia's Skills in Demand visa flags underestimating licensing requirements — occupational licensing tied to the specific role is an ongoing compliance matter, not a box checked once at the visa stage. Germany's EU Blue Card records leaving degree recognition until late as its own pitfall, separate from the salary threshold, because recognition can take real time to confirm and is not guaranteed. Vietnam's work permit route carries two related risks: assuming an exemption applies without actually checking it, and underestimating the lead time the whole process requires before someone can legally start work. Canada's LMIA guide adds a final, quieter version of the same risk: assuming the eligibility rules are stable, when the requirements around this route have changed materially in recent years, including the reduction of LMIA validity itself from 12 months to 6 for applications received on or after 1 May 2024.

## What this means for a specific application

None of these sixteen pitfalls are about an applicant's own qualifications being insufficient. Every one of them is about a condition outside the applicant's direct evidence — an employer's compliance, a role-specific threshold, a licence or recognition still pending — being treated as settled when it required active confirmation. Before assuming a work-intent application is strong because the applicant's own case is strong, the employer's obligations, the role-specific thresholds, and any outstanding licence or recognition step all need to be checked separately, on their own terms.
