## Sufficient is not the same question as sufficient, correctly held

A refusal or a return on financial grounds is often read by the applicant as "we did not have enough money," when the actual finding was narrower: the money existed, but not in the specific form the route required. Three separate, recorded requirements across this site's guides turn on exactly this distinction, and each fails a genuinely well-funded applicant in a different way.

## The account type itself is the requirement

Germany's student route does not accept a general bank balance as proof of funds — it requires a blocked account, a specific financial product that releases funds to the holder in monthly instalments rather than as a lump sum. Money genuinely available to a student, sitting in an ordinary account, does not satisfy this requirement no matter the amount, because the requirement is about the account structure, not the balance. This site's guide records underestimating the setup time for this account as its own pitfall — the account has to be established and funded correctly before the visa process can proceed, and a late realisation that an ordinary account will not do costs real time to fix.

## Held for the wrong period counts as not held at all

The UK's Student visa applies a stricter version of the same principle to timing rather than account type: the required maintenance funds must be held for a full, consecutive 28 days before applying, and if the balance drops below the threshold on even a single day inside that window, the 28-day clock resets from the beginning. Funds moved into the right account, in the right amount, the day before applying do not satisfy this requirement — the same money, moved four weeks earlier and left untouched, would.

## The clock moved, and the account did not know

Canada's study permit route shows a third version, and it is the one most likely to catch an otherwise careful applicant. The required financial threshold is a published figure that is revised, and this site's guide records budgeting to the previous year's figure as its own pitfall. An account genuinely holding what was, at some point, a sufficient amount can fall short of the current requirement without the applicant's financial position having changed at all — only the number it is being checked against.

## What "the wrong person's name" adds to this

Beyond account type and timing, several of these routes also specify whose funds count. A parent's or a scholarship provider's genuine, committed support does not automatically substitute for funds held in the applicant's own name, or a sponsor's, in the specific way the route defines — this varies by route and is exactly the kind of detail that needs to be confirmed directly rather than assumed from a general sense that "the money is there." A family with genuinely sufficient combined resources, held under the wrong person's name for that specific route's rules, can fail this requirement on paper despite having no real shortfall at all.

## What to check before assuming funds are ready

For any route with a financial requirement, the balance is the last thing to confirm, not the first. Before that: which account type or structure the route specifically requires, whether a holding period applies and whether it has been met without interruption, whether the published threshold has been checked against its current figure rather than an earlier one, and whose name the funds need to be held under. Genuinely sufficient money, held the wrong way, fails these checks exactly as often as insufficient money does — and it is a more frustrating failure, because it was avoidable with earlier planning rather than more funds.
