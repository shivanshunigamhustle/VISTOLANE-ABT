## The number measures a stage, not a journey

Every route on this site publishes, or would publish, a processing time — a figure meant to tell you how long a decision takes. The mistake almost everyone makes with these figures is treating them as the length of the whole process, start to finish. In practice a published processing time almost always measures one specific stage of a longer sequence, and the stages before it are where a plan built on the headline number usually runs into trouble.

Take Express Entry, one of the clearer examples on this site precisely because its service standard is stated plainly: 6 months for 80% of complete applications, measured from the day a complete application is received to the day a decision is made. That is a genuine, citable commitment. It is also explicitly scoped to start only after an invitation to apply has already been issued. The time a candidate spends sitting in the pool before that invitation — which can be substantial, and depends entirely on how their score compares to a moving cut-off — is not part of the 6 months and cannot be estimated from it.

## The preparation that happens before the clock starts

The Labour Market Impact Assessment route makes the same structure even more visible, because the preparation stage is not just uncounted, it is mandatory and lengthy. Before an employer can even submit an LMIA application, they must complete recruitment: for a high-wage position, at least three activities including a minimum of four consecutive weeks of advertising on the Government of Canada Job Bank, finishing within three months of applying. None of that period appears in the published LMIA processing time, which measures only Employment and Social Development Canada's own assessment once a complete application, evidence included, has been submitted. A plan that reads "the processing time is published as X" and stops there has quietly deleted several weeks that are certain to happen.

Study permits illustrate a different version of the same problem: a processing time that genuinely cannot be reduced to one number at all. It is published by country of application and revised frequently enough that this site's own study permit guide declines to state a figure, on the grounds that any single number would be wrong for most readers most of the time. That is not an evasion — publishing an average across every country of application would produce a number that describes almost nobody's actual case.

## Why "fixed" and "variable" service standards are not interchangeable

Not every processing figure behaves the same way, and it is worth knowing which kind you are looking at. The Global Talent Stream, a route within the LMIA system for select highly skilled occupations, has one of the few genuinely fixed commitments referenced across this site's guides: 10 business days for 80% of applications. That is a service standard an employer can plan a hiring timeline around with real confidence, precisely because it does not move with overall application volume the way most other figures do.

Most published processing times are not like that. They are usually a rolling measure — often stated as a figure covering some proportion of recent cases, commonly 80% — that shifts with how many applications the department is currently handling. A time published today can be materially different in three months, in either direction, without any change in policy at all. Treating a rolling figure as a fixed promise, the way it is reasonable to treat the Global Talent Stream's 10 business days, is a subtler version of the same mistake as ignoring the preparation stage.

## What a processing time cannot tell you about your own case

A published processing time is also, by construction, a statement about a batch of applications, not about a specific one. The 80%-style figures used across Express Entry and LMIA processing explicitly acknowledge that one in five cases falls outside the window quoted — which means a case that hits an unusual complication, a missing document, or simply bad timing can legitimately sit well outside the published figure without anything having gone wrong in the ordinary sense. A processing time is a reasonable planning baseline. It is not a guarantee for the specific file with your name on it.

## How to actually use one of these figures

Read a processing time as an answer to a narrower question than "how long will this take me": ask instead what stage it covers, whether it starts before or after your own preparation is complete, and whether it is a fixed service standard or a rolling figure that moves with volume. Then add back, deliberately, whatever precedes the stage it measures — an employer's recruitment period, a candidate's wait in the Express Entry pool, the time to obtain language results or a credential assessment — because that time is real even when no single published number includes it. The programme pages on this site try to make this explicit wherever a figure is confirmed and wherever it still needs checking against the current official source; reading both markers together is more useful than reading either headline number alone.
