## Same purpose, different product

A route being the closest match to what someone wants to do is not the same as it being the right category to apply under. Three of the countries this site covers record a version of this exact mistake, and in each case the two options being confused look similar enough on the surface that the error is easy to make honestly, not carelessly.

## Two visitor products under one label

Australia's Visitor visa (subclass 600) is not one product — it runs across several streams, including Tourist and Business Visitor, and this site's guide records applying for the wrong visitor option as a named pitfall in its own right. The streams carry different conditions, and choosing the one that sounds closest rather than the one that actually matches the visit's purpose can create a mismatch between what the visa permits and what the traveller intends to do.

Canada's Visitor Visa and Electronic Travel Authorization sit even closer together, since which one applies depends on nationality and mode of travel rather than a chosen category. This site's guide records applying for the wrong one as a distinct pitfall, alongside a related risk: assuming a decision on either is instant, when processing still takes real time regardless of which of the two actually applies.

## A tourist product used for a purpose it was never built for

Vietnam's routes show the sharpest version of this mistake. The e-visa is a short-stay tourism and business product; the DH visa is a distinct, dedicated category for study. This site's guide for Vietnam's student route records applying for a tourist e-visa instead of a DH visa as a named pitfall, and the underlying reason is structural, not a technicality: a short-stay category is not designed to support a genuine study stay, and using it for that purpose creates status risk once enrolled, however well-intentioned the choice was. The e-visa guide records the mirror-image mistake from the other direction — confusing the e-visa with visa-exemption, a separate arrangement that lets certain nationalities enter without any visa at all, which means applying for and paying for an e-visa that was never needed in the first place.

## Why "closest match" is the wrong test

In every case recorded here, the applicant was not attempting to use the wrong category to gain an advantage — the categories genuinely looked close enough to be mistaken for each other, and in Vietnam's case, close enough to justify the confusion by name alone (an electronic visa covering both tourism and some business activity, next to a study-specific document with a different letter code). The correct test is not which option sounds closest to the actual purpose of the trip, but which one is specifically built for that purpose under its own defined rules — checked against the route's own eligibility page, not inferred from a general description.
