## The application is the easy half

Every guide on this site treats the grant of a visa or permit as the outcome to plan for. Read the pitfalls recorded across all thirty routes this site covers and a different pattern shows up: a large share of them, sixteen of ninety-three, describe something going wrong after the grant, not before it. A window closes that nobody was tracking. A card that looked permanent turns out to need renewing. A status that felt settled was still conditional the whole time. None of these are edge cases restricted to one country — this shows up in every one of the five.

## The clock does not stop at approval

Several of the routes on this site build toward a later status over years, and the qualifying period is not a passive wait — it accrues correctly only if specific conditions hold throughout it. Germany's settlement permit is the clearest example: reaching it depends on language progression to at least B1, a Leben in Deutschland integration test, and a pension contribution record of around 60 months that builds quietly in the background of ordinary employment. A gap in that record — time in self-employment that did not contribute correctly, or a period outside standard employment — can leave an applicant short of the requirement at exactly the point they expected to qualify, without anything having gone visibly wrong along the way.

The UK's Indefinite Leave to Remain carries a related but stricter version: the 180-day absence rule is assessed on a rolling 12-month basis across the entire qualifying period, not year by year. A travel pattern that looks reasonable in any single year can still breach the rule when checked on a rolling basis, and the check only happens at the point of application — by which time it is too late to have travelled differently.

## A residence permit and a residence card are not the same promise

Vietnam's routes make a distinction this site's guides keep returning to: a visa gets someone in the door, and a residence card is a separate, later document that has to be actively applied for once inside the country — it does not arrive automatically. The Permanent Residence Card is renewed every 10 years even though the underlying status is indefinite, which is easy to overlook precisely because the status itself does not expire. The Family (TT) visa follows the same shape: the initial visa runs up to 12 months, and a family member intending to stay longer has to plan toward the residence card stage from the outset rather than assume the visa itself is the long-term answer. Vietnam's investor route repeats the pattern again — a DT visa on its own is not the long-term outcome; the temporary residence card that can follow it is, and applying for that card is a distinct step after arrival.

## "Temporary" sometimes means exactly that, and nothing more

Vietnam's work permit route states this most directly among the pitfalls recorded here: the permit is not permanent, and treating it as though it were is one of the recorded ways this route goes wrong. Germany's student route carries a version of the same lesson from the opposite direction — the national (D) visa a student arrives on is not the end of the process, and further steps at the residence-permit stage still have to happen once inside the country, not assumed to follow automatically from the visa. A student in Vietnam is expected to register their address with local police shortly after arriving, a separate administrative step from the visa itself.

## Cover that has to stay in force, not just exist at the start

Two routes flag a subtler version of the same problem: a requirement that has to remain true throughout the stay, not merely at the moment of application. Australia's Student visa lists letting English test results or health cover lapse as a recorded pitfall — evidence gathered once for the application is not necessarily evidence that stays valid for its duration. Australia's Skills in Demand visa carries a related risk on the employment side: licensing requirements tied to the specific occupation are an ongoing compliance matter for as long as the person is working in that role, not a box checked once at the visa stage.

Every one of these facts already lives on its own programme page, footnoted with its own source and its own "Verify before publish" marker where the detail was not independently confirmable. What does not exist anywhere else on this site is the pattern across them: obligations that continue, and sometimes intensify, after the part most applicants plan hardest for is already finished. Reading the individual programme guide for a specific route is still the right place to check the exact figures. This page exists so the shape of the risk is visible before an applicant gets there — so that "the visa arrived" is read as the middle of the process, not the end of it.
