## Three different questions, all called "investor"

It is worth saying plainly: the routes on this site labelled for investors do not all ask the same question. Vietnam's DT visa asks how much capital was contributed, in a defined tier structure. Australia's National Innovation Visa asks whether the applicant has an internationally recognised record of exceptional achievement and can earn above a high income threshold; capital is not the test at all. The UK's Innovator Founder visa and Canada's Start-up Visa both ask a third, different question: will a specific third-party organisation vouch for this business. Treating these as interchangeable, or assuming a large amount of available capital is the main qualification for all of them, is the most consequential misunderstanding an applicant researching this intent can carry into a real application.

## Pure capital: Vietnam's tiered model

Vietnam's DT visa is the most literal of the five, eligibility and outcome are set directly by investment size, split into four tiers reported at VND 100 billion or more (DT1), VND 50 to under 100 billion (DT2), VND 3 to under 50 billion (DT3), and under VND 3 billion (DT4). The tier is not just a label; it determines whether a temporary residence card is available at all afterward. DT1 and DT2 are reported leading to residence cards of up to 10 and 5 years respectively; DT4, the smallest tier, is reported as having no card option, leaving the investor renewing a 12-month visa annually. This is capital-based immigration in its most direct form, the harder question is not whether the capital qualifies, but which side of a tier boundary it lands on.

## Achievement, not capital: Australia's National Innovation Visa

Australia is the clearest counterexample to "investor visa means capital." Its National Innovation Visa replaced a genuinely capital-based programme, the former Business Innovation and Investment visa, including subclass 188 and 132, which closed to new applications on 31 July 2024, with a route assessed on an internationally recognised record of exceptional achievement and the capacity to earn above a high income threshold. An applicant with substantial capital but no comparable track record of achievement does not fit this route's actual test, regardless of how the word "investor" might suggest otherwise. Anyone working from older guidance describing Australia's investor route in capital terms is working from a programme that no longer exists in that form.

## Third-party endorsement: the UK and Canada

The UK's Innovator Founder visa and Canada's Start-up Visa share a structure neither Vietnam's nor Australia's route has: a required endorsement from an approved third-party organisation before the government application even begins. In the UK, that is a Home Office approved endorsing body assessing the business for innovation, viability and scalability; a commercial assessment, not a formality, that can and does result in a declined endorsement for a weak business case. In Canada, the Start-up Visa requires support from a designated organisation in a broadly similar spirit. On both routes, the practical difficulty sits upstream of the visa application itself: a strong business plan capable of persuading a specific third-party assessor matters more than the size of the applicant's personal capital.

## Discretionary and needs-based: Germany's self-employment route

Germany's self-employment residence permit is a fourth pattern again; assessed on an economic interest or regional need for the specific business, its expected positive economic impact, secure financing, and the applicant's relevant qualifications and experience. There is no fixed capital threshold reported for this route the way there is for Vietnam's DT tiers; the assessment is more discretionary, weighing the business case against Germany's own economic interest rather than checking it against a number.

## What this means before starting an application

Before researching "investor visa" as a single category, identify which of these four patterns the target country's route actually follows, a fixed capital threshold, an achievement and income test, a third-party endorsement, or a discretionary economic-benefit assessment, because the preparation each demands is genuinely different. A strong business plan is the right preparation for the UK or Canada; a documented track record of achievement is the right preparation for Australia; capital placed correctly against a tier boundary is the right preparation for Vietnam. Preparing for the wrong one of these wastes real time on the wrong evidence.
