## Two routes, one decision

Canada does not ask a short-term visitor to choose between an eTA and a Temporary Resident Visa — nationality decides it. Citizens of a defined list of countries use the eTA: a short online form, a $7 fee, and in most cases a decision within minutes. Everyone else applies for a TRV, a longer process involving supporting documents, biometrics at a visa application centre, and a wait that can run to weeks or months depending on where the application is made.

That split matters for how this route should be planned around. An eTA is close enough to instant that it barely needs advance planning. A TRV is the opposite — it is the kind of process that punishes anyone who books non-refundable travel before a decision arrives.

## Who this suits, and who it does not

It suits anyone making a genuine short visit — tourism, seeing family, attending a business meeting that stops short of taking up employment. It does not suit anyone hoping to work while in Canada; neither the eTA nor the TRV authorises employment, and using either for that purpose is a real compliance problem, not a grey area.

For an eTA-eligible traveller, this is about as low-friction as immigration paperwork gets. For a TRV applicant, it suits patience and early planning far better than it suits anyone working to a tight travel date.

## What actually gets assessed

An eTA is largely a passport and identity check. A TRV asks a more open question: will this person actually leave Canada at the end of the visit. That is why a TRV application leans on evidence of ties elsewhere — a job, property, family — rather than only on the stated purpose of the trip. Two applicants with an identical travel itinerary can have different outcomes if one of them presents materially weaker evidence of ties to their home country.
