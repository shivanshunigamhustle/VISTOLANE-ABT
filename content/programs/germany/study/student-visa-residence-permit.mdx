## What this route actually is

Studying in Germany as a non-EU/EEA national runs through two stages, not one: a national (D) visa obtained before travel, and a residence permit for studying obtained after arrival, at the local foreigners' registration office. The D visa gets a student into the country; it is the residence permit that actually covers the length of the course.

The financial-proof mechanism is distinctive too. Rather than a simple bank statement, most applicants use a blocked account; a specific type of account that releases a capped monthly amount, currently reported around EUR 992, against a required deposit currently reported at EUR 11,904. The structure exists specifically so a student cannot spend down a lump sum immediately after arrival and be left without support later in the year.

## Who this suits, and who it does not

It suits an applicant with confirmed university admission who starts the financial and administrative preparation early; particularly the blocked account, which takes real time to open and fund and sits on the critical path to the visa application itself. It suits it less well anyone hoping to move quickly close to a course start date; between blocked account setup and national visa processing, this is not a fast-turnaround route.

A fully funded scholarship that explicitly covers living costs and health insurance can remove the need for a blocked account, but a partial scholarship generally does not; this distinction is worth checking carefully rather than assumed in either direction.
